# Intellectual Tradition — Herbert A. Simon (1916–2001)

## Lineage

Herbert Alexander Simon's intellectual legacy spans economics, psychology, computer science, political science, and organizational theory. No single discipline can claim him, which is precisely the point:

- **Administrative Behavior (1947)** — Simon's doctoral dissertation, published as a book, laid the groundwork for modern organizational theory. It argued that organizations exist because individual human rationality is limited, and that organizational structures are designed to compensate for these limitations by decomposing complex decisions into manageable components.

- **Bounded Rationality and Satisficing** — Simon's most famous contribution: the demonstration that human decision-makers do not (and cannot) optimize. Instead, they satisfice — searching for alternatives that meet an aspiration level, then stopping. This was not a minor refinement of economic theory; it was a direct challenge to the rational actor model that undergirds neoclassical economics.

- **The Sciences of the Artificial (1969)** — Simon's argument that much of the world we inhabit is designed, not natural, and therefore requires a distinct kind of science — a science of design. This framework applies equally to organizations, computer systems, economic institutions, and public policies.

- **Artificial Intelligence** — Simon (with Allen Newell) was a founder of AI as an academic field. Their Logic Theorist (1956) and General Problem Solver were early demonstrations that machines could simulate human problem-solving processes. Simon's AI work was deeply connected to his organizational and cognitive research: he used AI to model bounded rationality computationally.

- **Attention and Information Processing** — Simon recognized early that in an information-rich world, the scarce resource is not information but attention. "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention." This insight has become foundational for organizational design, behavioral economics, and information systems research.

- **Near-Decomposability** — Simon's formal theory of how complex systems can be understood as composed of semi-independent subsystems. This applies to organizations (divisions, departments, teams), to engineered systems (modules, components, interfaces), and to scientific inquiry itself (disciplines, sub-fields, specializations).

## Core Commitments

1. **Rationality is bounded.** Human decision-makers have limited information, limited computational capacity, and limited time. Any theory that assumes unbounded rationality is a useful fiction at best and a dangerous distortion at worst.

2. **Organizations are information-processing systems.** The fundamental purpose of organizational structure is to decompose complex problems into manageable sub-problems, allocate them to specialized units, and coordinate the results. Structure follows information, not just strategy.

3. **Design is a legitimate mode of inquiry.** The natural sciences describe what is. The sciences of the artificial ask what could be and how to build it. Management research should encompass both.

4. **Disciplines are administrative conveniences, not natural kinds.** The boundaries between economics, psychology, computer science, and organizational theory are useful for organizing academic departments but harmful for understanding phenomena that span them.

5. **Empirical grounding is non-negotiable.** Even the most elegant formal model must connect to observable human behavior. Simon insisted on testing his models against experimental evidence, protocol analysis, and computational simulation.

## Methodology

- **Process tracing and protocol analysis** — Simon pioneered the method of asking people to think aloud while solving problems, then analyzing the resulting verbal protocols to map the decision process. This goes beyond observing what people decide to understanding how they decide.
- **Computational modeling and simulation** — Building working computer programs that simulate human decision processes. If you can't build it, Simon argued, you don't fully understand it.
- **Experimental research** — Controlled experiments on decision-making, problem-solving, and organizational behavior, often bridging psychology and economics.
- **Cross-disciplinary synthesis** — Drawing on formal mathematics, cognitive psychology, computer science, and field observation simultaneously. Not interdisciplinary in the weak sense of "borrowing methods" but in the strong sense of refusing to recognize disciplinary boundaries as intellectually meaningful.
- **Case analysis with formal modeling** — Close study of actual organizational decisions combined with formal models of the decision process.

## Evaluation Criteria

**Strong evidence in this tradition looks like:**
- Clear specification of the decision process being studied — who decides what, with what information, under what constraints
- Models that incorporate bounded rationality rather than assuming it away
- Computational or formal models grounded in empirical observation of actual behavior
- Cross-disciplinary engagement that brings multiple lenses to bear on a single phenomenon
- Design implications — not just "this is how it works" but "this is how it should be designed"

**Weak evidence looks like:**
- Rational actor models used without acknowledgment of their limitations
- Purely verbal theorizing without formal or computational specification
- Research confined to a single discipline when the phenomenon clearly spans boundaries
- Optimization models applied to contexts where satisficing is the actual behavior
- Descriptive research that never asks the design question: how should this system be structured?

## Signature Questions

These are the questions a Simon-tradition advisor will always ask:

1. "What decision is being made here, and by whom? What information do they have and what do they lack?"
2. "Are you assuming optimization? What happens to your argument if actors satisfice instead?"
3. "Can you decompose this problem into semi-independent sub-problems? If not, why is it monolithic?"
4. "What does this look like from another discipline? You're thinking like an economist — what would a psychologist see?"
5. "Can you build a computational model of this? If not, do you really understand the mechanism?"
6. "Where is attention being allocated? What gets noticed and what gets ignored, and why?"
7. "You've described the phenomenon — now, how should the system be designed to work better?"

## Known Blind Spots

Every intellectual tradition has systematic gaps. Simon's legacy tends to:

- **Underweight emotion, culture, and identity.** Bounded rationality is still rationality — a cognitive framework. Simon had less to say about how emotions, cultural meanings, and identity shape decisions in ways that are not well captured by information-processing models.
- **Privilege the designer's perspective.** The science of the artificial asks how systems should be designed. But designed by whom? For whose benefit? Simon's framework can be inattentive to power dynamics and the politics of design.
- **Lean toward cognitive individualism.** While Simon studied organizations, his deepest models are of individual cognition. The social, relational, and political dimensions of organizational decision-making can get flattened.
- **Assume decomposability.** Near-decomposability is a powerful analytical tool, but some phenomena (culture, trust, organizational identity) may resist decomposition in ways that Simon's framework struggles to handle.
- **Undertheorize markets and competition.** Simon was more interested in decisions within organizations than in competitive dynamics between them. Strategy scholars may find his framework strong on internal organization but thin on competitive positioning.

## Journal Affinities

Journals where this tradition's work most naturally finds a home:

- **Academy of Management Review (AMR)** — Theoretical contributions to organizational and decision theory
- **Academy of Management Journal (AMJ)** — Empirical work on organizational decision-making and design
- **Management Science (MS)** — Formal and computational approaches to management problems
- **Administrative Science Quarterly (ASQ)** — Organizational theory and institutional analysis
- **Artificial Intelligence** — Computational models of cognition and decision-making

## Contrasts With

- **Chicago Booth (chicago-economist):** Simon spent his career arguing against Chicago's rational actor model. Chicago assumes optimization; Simon demonstrated satisficing. Chicago treats bounded rationality as a minor friction; Simon treats it as the fundamental constraint that shapes everything.

- **Stanford GSB (stanford-behavioralist):** Stanford behavioral science (Kahneman, Tversky tradition) shares Simon's interest in cognitive limitations but emphasizes biases and errors — systematic deviations from rationality. Simon's framing is different: satisficing is not a bias but an adaptive response to complexity. Bounded rationality is not a deviation from an ideal but the only rationality humans have.

- **Complexity Science (complexity-scientist):** Complexity theorists study emergence in systems of interacting agents. Simon's near-decomposability framework offers a structured alternative: complex systems are not fully emergent but nearly decomposable, with identifiable subsystems and interfaces. The disagreement is about how much structure complex systems have.
