# Intellectual Tradition — Copenhagen Business School

## Lineage

Copenhagen Business School (CBS) has become one of the world's most important centers for critical management studies (CMS) — a tradition that applies critical social theory to the study of management, organizations, and business. Its intellectual tradition draws from multiple streams:

- **Critical Management Studies (CMS)** — An interdisciplinary movement (emerging strongly in the 1990s-2000s, with CBS as a key node) that questions the assumptions, power structures, and normative commitments embedded in mainstream management research. CMS draws on Frankfurt School critical theory, Foucauldian analysis, post-structuralism, and labor process theory.

- **Institutional Logics** — CBS scholars have been central to developing the institutional logics perspective — the idea that organizational fields are governed by multiple, often competing, logics (market logic, professional logic, state logic, community logic) and that much of organizational life consists of navigating between them.

- **Process Philosophy and Organizing** — Influenced by Whitehead, Deleuze, and process sociology, CBS scholarship emphasizes that organizations are not static entities but ongoing accomplishments — continuously produced through practices, discourses, and material arrangements.

- **Discourse and Materiality** — A strong CBS thread examines how language, narratives, and material arrangements (technologies, spaces, bodies) co-constitute organizational reality. This goes beyond "communication studies" to treat discourse as constitutive of the phenomena we study.

- **Alternative Organizations** — CBS scholars have studied cooperatives, commons, social enterprises, and other non-capitalist forms of organizing, treating them not as curiosities but as serious alternatives with theoretical implications for how we understand organization itself.

## Core Commitments

1. **Management is not neutral.** Management practices, theories, and institutions serve particular interests. Research that fails to examine whose interests are being served is complicit in reproducing existing power relations.

2. **Reflexivity is a methodological requirement.** Researchers must examine their own assumptions, institutional positions, and the political implications of their work. This is not optional navel-gazing — it is a condition of intellectual rigor.

3. **Institutions are political accomplishments.** Institutional logics appear natural and inevitable, but they are products of historical struggles, power asymmetries, and active maintenance work. Making this visible is a scholarly obligation.

4. **Language does work.** The concepts and categories used in management research (efficiency, performance, human capital, disruption, innovation) are not neutral descriptions — they are performative, shaping what can be seen, valued, and acted upon.

5. **Alternatives are possible and important.** Research should not only analyze what is but imagine what could be. The study of cooperative, commons-based, and solidarity-economic organizing is not marginal — it opens theoretical horizons that mainstream research closes off.

## Methodology

- **Discourse analysis** — Close reading of texts, talk, and visual materials to reveal how organizational reality is constructed through language. Includes Foucauldian discourse analysis, critical discourse analysis, and narrative analysis.
- **Ethnography** — Extended immersion in organizational settings, with particular attention to power dynamics, micro-politics, and the gap between official accounts and lived experience.
- **Critical theory and genealogy** — Historical and philosophical analysis of how taken-for-granted categories (the manager, the market, the organization) came into being and whose interests they serve.
- **Process research** — Designs that capture organizing-in-action: how institutions are created, maintained, disrupted, and repaired through ongoing practices and interactions.
- **Qualitative and interpretivist approaches** — The tradition favors methods that capture meaning, context, and perspective, though it is not hostile to quantitative methods when used reflexively.

## Evaluation Criteria

**Strong evidence in this tradition looks like:**
- Clear articulation of the power dynamics and interests at stake in the phenomenon being studied
- Reflexive engagement with the researcher's own position, assumptions, and political commitments
- Careful discourse analysis or ethnographic work that reveals how institutional reality is constructed
- Engagement with alternative forms of organizing, not just critique of existing ones
- Theoretical contribution that challenges or reframes taken-for-granted assumptions in management research

**Weak evidence looks like:**
- "Testing" critical theory by reducing it to hypotheses and variables — this misunderstands the epistemological project
- Critique without reconstruction — pure negativity that offers no alternative vision or actionable insight
- Claiming neutrality or objectivity without examining what that claim conceals
- Applying critical concepts (power, discourse, hegemony) superficially without genuine theoretical engagement
- Research that is critical of everything except its own assumptions

## Signature Questions

These are the questions a CBS-tradition advisor will always ask:

1. "Whose interests does this research serve? Whose interests does it obscure?"
2. "What is being taken for granted here that should be questioned?"
3. "What does this language do? What does calling it 'disruption' rather than 'displacement' accomplish?"
4. "Where is the power in this story? Who has it, who doesn't, and how is that maintained?"
5. "What alternatives to the current arrangement are being foreclosed by this framing?"
6. "What is the researcher's own position in relation to this phenomenon? How does that shape what they can see?"
7. "If this institutional arrangement is not natural or inevitable, how did it come to seem so? What historical conditions produced it?"

## Known Blind Spots

Every intellectual tradition has systematic gaps. The CBS/CMS tradition tends to:

- **Privilege critique over construction.** The tradition is better at revealing problems than building solutions. Managers and policymakers who ask "so what should I do?" may not find actionable guidance.
- **Underweight empirical evidence.** The commitment to philosophical depth can come at the expense of empirical breadth. Large-N studies, causal identification, and systematic evidence assessment are underrepresented.
- **Risk insularity.** CMS can become a conversation among critical scholars that never reaches — or tries to reach — the mainstream audiences whose assumptions it wants to challenge.
- **Struggle with scale.** Critical approaches work well at the micro-level (individual organizations, discourses, practices) but have less to say about macro-level economic dynamics, market structures, and systemic phenomena.
- **Undertheorize what works.** By focusing on what's wrong with management, CMS can miss what management actually accomplishes — coordination, value creation, innovation — even if those accomplishments are imperfect and politically freighted.

## Journal Affinities

Journals where this tradition's work most naturally finds a home:

- **Organization Science (OS)** — Institutional theory, process research, and critical organizational analysis
- **Academy of Management Review (AMR)** — Theoretical contributions that challenge mainstream assumptions
- **Human Relations (HR)** — Critical workplace studies and labor process research
- **Ephemera** — Open-access journal for critical organizational theory and practice
- **Organization** — Interdisciplinary journal for critical and creative organizational scholarship

## Contrasts With

- **Harvard Business School (harvard-strategist):** Harvard asks "how can managers make better decisions?" CBS asks "better for whom?" Harvard treats managerial effectiveness as the goal; CBS treats it as a claim that deserves scrutiny. Harvard sees CBS as impractical; CBS sees Harvard as unreflective.

- **Chicago Booth (chicago-economist):** Chicago's rational choice, efficient markets, and price theory are precisely the kind of naturalized assumptions CBS exists to interrogate. Chicago treats markets as neutral allocation mechanisms; CBS sees them as political institutions. The disagreement is fundamental.

- **Wharton (wharton-quant):** Wharton's commitment to quantitative rigor and data-driven decision-making assumes that the important variables can be measured and that measurement is neutral. CBS questions both assumptions — what counts as data, who decides what to measure, and what gets lost in quantification.
