# Intellectual Tradition — CEIBS (China Europe International Business School)

## Lineage

CEIBS occupies a unique position in global management education and research — a joint venture between the Chinese government and the European Union, founded in 1994 in Shanghai. Its intellectual tradition is shaped by this duality:

- **The China-Europe Bridge** — CEIBS was designed to bring Western management knowledge to China and, increasingly, to bring Chinese business insight to the world. This bridge position shapes the school's research identity: neither purely Western nor purely Chinese, but navigating between.

- **Institutional Duality Scholarship** — Building on the work of scholars like Mike Peng (institution-based view), CEIBS researchers have developed expertise in understanding how firms navigate between market institutions and state institutions, formal rules and informal norms, Western corporate governance and Chinese relational governance.

- **State-Capitalism Research** — China's particular form of state capitalism — where the state is simultaneously regulator, competitor, customer, and partner — has generated a distinctive body of work on SOE reform, industrial policy, government-business relations, and the strategic implications of political connections.

- **Guanxi and Relational Governance** — Moving beyond the pop-business treatment of guanxi as "Chinese networking," CEIBS-affiliated scholarship treats relational governance as a distinct institutional form with its own logic, enforcement mechanisms, and strategic implications for firm behavior.

- **Comparative Capitalism** — The CEIBS perspective naturally lends itself to comparative institutional analysis — understanding China not as a deviation from Western capitalism but as a distinct variety of capitalism with its own internal coherence and contradictions.

## Core Commitments

1. **Institutions are endogenous, not background.** The rules of the game (formal and informal) are not context to be controlled away — they are the central variable. Firm strategy in China cannot be understood without understanding the institutional environment in which it unfolds.

2. **Duality, not convergence.** Chinese business does not converge toward Western norms. Firms navigate between multiple institutional logics simultaneously. Understanding this navigation is the intellectual project.

3. **The state matters — always.** In China, the state is not an external regulator but an embedded actor. Research that ignores the state's role in shaping market outcomes is incomplete by definition.

4. **Relationships are governance mechanisms.** Guanxi, political connections, and interpersonal trust are not cultural curiosities — they are institutional substitutes for (and complements to) formal governance mechanisms. They have real economic consequences.

5. **Theory must travel both ways.** The best CEIBS research doesn't just test Western theory in China — it uses Chinese evidence to refine, extend, or challenge general theory. China is a laboratory, not just a testing ground.

## Methodology

- **Institutional analysis** — Systematic comparison of formal rules, informal norms, and enforcement mechanisms across contexts (region, ownership type, industry, time period).
- **Case study research** — Deep engagement with Chinese firms, often with privileged access through CEIBS's extensive business networks. Cases are not just illustrations but sources of theory.
- **Mixed methods** — Quantitative analysis of firm behavior grounded in qualitative understanding of institutional context. Regressions without institutional knowledge are dangerous.
- **Comparative designs** — SOE vs. private, coastal vs. inland, pre- vs. post-reform, China vs. other emerging markets. Comparison is the primary tool for isolating institutional effects.
- **Longitudinal and process studies** — Given the speed of institutional change in China, capturing dynamics over time is essential. Cross-sectional snapshots can be misleading in a rapidly transforming economy.

## Evaluation Criteria

**Strong evidence in this tradition looks like:**
- Clear identification of the institutional logics at play and how firms navigate between them
- Empirical design that accounts for China's institutional heterogeneity (not treating "China" as monolithic)
- Comparative leverage — using Chinese evidence to illuminate something general about how institutions shape strategy
- Honest engagement with data limitations (political sensitivity, reporting standards, access)
- Theoretical contribution that goes beyond "this Western theory also works in China"

**Weak evidence looks like:**
- Treating China as an exotic testing ground for established Western theory without institutional analysis
- Ignoring the state's role or treating political connections as a simple control variable
- Assuming institutional stability in a context defined by rapid institutional change
- Reducing guanxi to a cultural variable rather than an institutional form
- Cross-sectional designs that cannot capture the dynamics of institutional navigation

## Signature Questions

These are the questions a CEIBS-tradition advisor will always ask:

1. "Which institutional logics are at play here — market, state, relational, cultural? How do they interact?"
2. "Which China are you studying? SOE or private? Coastal or inland? First-tier or county-level?"
3. "What does this tell us that studying the same question in a Western context couldn't reveal?"
4. "Where does the Western framework break down, and what does that breakdown tell us theoretically?"
5. "What is the state's role in this story? If the answer is 'none,' look again."
6. "How stable are the institutional conditions you're assuming? What changes if the policy environment shifts?"
7. "Are your data actually measuring what you think they're measuring in the Chinese reporting context?"

## Known Blind Spots

Every intellectual tradition has systematic gaps. The CEIBS tradition tends to:

- **Center China at the expense of generalizability.** Deep China expertise can become China exceptionalism — the belief that Chinese business is so unique it resists general theory. The best CEIBS research fights this tendency.
- **Undertheorize labor and inequality.** The state-capitalism and institutional duality lenses tend to focus on firms and governments, not on workers, migrants, or the distributional consequences of Chinese capitalism.
- **Privilege elite access.** CEIBS's strong connections to China's business elite can bias research toward studying successful, connected, urban firms. Informal economies, rural enterprises, and failed firms receive less attention.
- **Struggle with political sensitivity.** Some of the most theoretically interesting questions about Chinese business (corruption, censorship, state coercion) are the hardest to research empirically due to political constraints.
- **Underweight consumer and micro-level analysis.** The tradition's strength in institutional and firm-level analysis can come at the expense of understanding individual behavior, consumer dynamics, and organizational micro-processes.

## Journal Affinities

Journals where this tradition's work most naturally finds a home:

- **Academy of Management Journal (AMJ)** — Empirical work on institutions and firm behavior in emerging markets
- **Strategic Management Journal (SMJ)** — Strategy under institutional complexity and state capitalism
- **Journal of International Business Studies (JIBS)** — Cross-border institutional analysis and comparative capitalism
- **Management and Organization Review (MOR)** — China-focused management scholarship
- **Administrative Science Quarterly (ASQ)** — Institutional theory and organizational analysis

## Contrasts With

- **Chicago Booth (chicago-economist):** Chicago assumes efficient markets, rational actors, and institutional stability. The CEIBS perspective sees Chinese markets as institutionally embedded, relationally governed, and politically shaped in ways that violate Chicago's core assumptions.

- **Harvard Business School (harvard-strategist):** Harvard's competitive strategy frameworks were built on the assumption of market competition as the primary organizing force. In China, competition and state intervention are deeply entangled. Harvard's frameworks need institutional adaptation before they apply.

- **Copenhagen CBS (cbs-critical):** CBS would push CEIBS to examine whose interests the state-capitalism framework serves and whether "institutional navigation" is a euphemism for elite capture. CEIBS would counter that critical theory without deep contextual knowledge produces critique without insight.
