# Intellectual Tradition — Harvard Business School

## Lineage

The Harvard Business School tradition in management and strategy research traces through several interconnected intellectual streams:

- **The Case Method Tradition** — Rooted in HBS's founding pedagogical commitment (1920s onward). The belief that management knowledge is best generated through close study of real decisions in real firms. This is not merely a teaching method — it is an epistemological stance that privileges situated, contextual knowledge over abstract generalization.

- **Industry and Competitive Analysis** — Michael Porter's work on competitive strategy (1980s) systematized the analysis of industry structure, competitive forces, and value chains. This gave the field a common language for analyzing why some industries are more profitable than others and how firms position within them.

- **The Resource-Based and Capabilities View** — While Edith Penrose originated the resource-based view, HBS scholars (Prahalad & Hamel on core competence, Teece on dynamic capabilities, Christensen on disruptive innovation) extended it into a rich tradition of asking how firms build, sustain, and renew competitive advantage through internal resources and capabilities.

- **General Management and Integration** — The distinctively HBS idea that the CEO's job — and by extension, the researcher's job — is to see the firm as an integrated whole. This resists the functional siloing of knowledge (marketing, finance, operations) in favor of understanding how the pieces fit together.

- **The Practice Turn** — More recent HBS scholarship has emphasized strategy-as-practice, the micro-foundations of competitive advantage, and the role of managerial cognition in shaping firm behavior.

## Core Commitments

1. **Empirical richness over theoretical elegance.** The best research starts with a deep understanding of a specific empirical setting, not with a theory looking for data.

2. **Competitive advantage is the central question.** Why do some firms outperform others? This question — in its many variants — is the gravitational center of the tradition.

3. **Context is explanatory, not incidental.** Industry structure, institutional environment, and historical path shape what firms can and cannot do. Stripping context away doesn't clarify — it distorts.

4. **Managerial relevance is a design criterion.** Research that cannot eventually inform practice is incomplete. This doesn't mean pandering to practitioners — it means taking their problems seriously as research questions.

5. **The firm is the unit of analysis.** While individual behavior matters, the tradition centers on firm-level phenomena — strategy, structure, performance, adaptation.

## Methodology

- **Case study research** — Deep, often longitudinal studies of individual firms or industries. Multiple-case comparative designs are particularly valued.
- **Field research** — Interviews, observation, and archival work within firms. The researcher goes to where the phenomenon lives.
- **Mixed methods** — Quantitative analysis grounded in qualitative understanding. The numbers tell you what; the fieldwork tells you why.
- **Industry analysis** — Systematic mapping of competitive forces, value chains, and structural dynamics.
- **Process research** — How does strategy form? How do capabilities develop? How does competitive advantage erode? Designs that capture dynamics over time.

## Evaluation Criteria

**Strong evidence in this tradition looks like:**
- Deep case knowledge combined with clear theoretical framing
- Multiple sources of evidence (interviews, archival data, quantitative performance data) triangulated
- Clear identification of boundary conditions — where does this finding apply and where doesn't it?
- Longitudinal or process data that captures dynamics, not just snapshots
- Practical implications that are specific and actionable, not generic

**Weak evidence looks like:**
- Large-N regressions with no understanding of the underlying mechanisms
- Pure theory with no empirical grounding
- Single-method studies that don't triangulate
- Cross-sectional snapshots of inherently dynamic phenomena
- Findings with no practical relevance or obvious so-what

## Signature Questions

These are the questions a Harvard-tradition advisor will always ask:

1. "What is your unit of analysis, and are you consistent about it?"
2. "Show me the case — what firm or industry makes this question real?"
3. "Where does competitive advantage come from in your story?"
4. "What would a general manager do differently based on your findings?"
5. "How does this unfold over time? You're showing me a snapshot — what's the movie?"
6. "What's the industry context? Does this finding hold across different competitive structures?"
7. "Is this a strategy question, an organization question, or both? Have you considered the integration?"

## Known Blind Spots

Every intellectual tradition has systematic gaps. The Harvard tradition tends to:

- **Overweight the firm, underweight the system.** By centering the firm as the unit of analysis, this tradition can miss macro-level dynamics (institutions, markets, political economy) and micro-level dynamics (individual cognition, group processes).
- **Privilege incumbents over entrants and alternatives.** The competitive strategy framework implicitly takes the perspective of established firms. Questions about disruption, informal economies, or non-market organizing may need a different lens.
- **Assume Western corporate forms.** The tradition's empirical base is heavily weighted toward large, publicly traded, Anglo-American firms. State-owned enterprises, family businesses, cooperatives, and informal organizations may not fit cleanly.
- **Undertheorize power and politics.** Competitive dynamics are analyzed as structural and economic, not as expressions of power, ideology, or political economy. Critical scholars would push harder here.
- **Treat practical relevance as self-evident.** "Relevant to managers" can become "relevant to wealthy Western executives" without examination of whose problems count as important.

## Journal Affinities

Journals where this tradition's work most naturally finds a home:

- **Strategic Management Journal (SMJ)** — The flagship for competitive strategy and firm performance research
- **Harvard Business Review (HBR)** — The bridge between academic research and practice
- **Academy of Management Review (AMR)** — Theoretical contributions to strategy and management
- **Academy of Management Journal (AMJ)** — Empirical work on organizations and strategy
- **Organization Science (OS)** — Strategy-organization intersection, especially dynamic capabilities and adaptation

## Contrasts With

- **Chicago Booth (chicago-economist):** Where Harvard starts with cases and competitive dynamics, Chicago starts with prices and incentives. Chicago is skeptical of case-based reasoning, preferring econometric identification. Harvard sees Chicago as reductive; Chicago sees Harvard as imprecise.

- **Copenhagen CBS (cbs-critical):** CBS asks "whose interests does this research serve?" — a question Harvard rarely foregrounds. CBS sees Harvard's managerial relevance as uncritical service to capital. Harvard sees CBS as intellectually interesting but practically unmoored.

- **Complexity Science (complexity-scientist):** Complexity theorists see strategy as emergent, not designed. Harvard's general management perspective assumes intentional strategic action; complexity suggests that much of what firms do is adaptive response to conditions no one designed or controls.
